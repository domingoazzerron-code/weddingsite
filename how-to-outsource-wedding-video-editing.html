Guide for wedding videographers

How to outsource wedding video editing

Editing is where most wedding videographers lose their weekends. Here is how to hand it off without losing your style, your clients or your sleep.

By Marcus Wedding Studio · Updated November 2026

Why videographers outsource their editing

Shooting a wedding takes a day. Editing it takes many times longer. Outsourcing the edit lets you take more weddings in peak season, deliver faster, and spend your time on the parts only you can do: meeting couples, shooting and growing the business.

Signs it’s time to outsource

  • Your delivery dates keep slipping, or couples are chasing you for their film.
  • You turn down bookings because your edit queue is full.
  • You spend your evenings and days off syncing, sorting and cutting.
  • You want to try a style or a package you don’t have time to build yourself.

What to hand off, and what to keep

Most videographers keep the client relationship, the shoot and the creative direction, and hand off the production work: syncing, sorting, cutting, color grading and building the teaser, highlights or full film. You stay the face of the brand, and the editor works as part of your team behind it.

Start with one deliverable, usually the teaser or the highlight film, and add the rest once you trust the process.

How to choose a wedding video editor

  • Relevant work. Look for wedding films, not only general video. Check how they handle pacing, speeches and music.
  • A clear process. You should know how you send footage, how drafts reach you, how feedback works and when you pay.
  • Honest turnaround and pricing. A good editor tells you what drives the price (footage, cameras, deliverable, deadline) and how long it takes.
  • Communication. Fast, clear replies matter more than a polished website.
  • Respect for your footage. Ask how files are handled and who sees them.

Test before you commit.

The safest way to hire an editor is a short test on your own footage. Send a few minutes, ask for a 30–60 second edit, and judge the result against your style. At Marcus Wedding Studio, new clients can request this test edit for a minimal charge.

How to keep your style when someone else edits

Your editor can’t read your mind, but they can follow a good brief. Send two or three reference films you’ve made, say what you like in each, and note things like pacing, how much speech to keep, and your color look. Sharing a LUT, your logo and your title style helps a lot. After the first project, most of this carries over, and each edit gets closer to what you want.

How pricing usually works

Editors usually quote per project. The price depends on how much footage and how many cameras you shot, which deliverable you need (teaser, highlights, full film, ceremony and speeches), and how fast you need it. Rush delivery usually costs more. Ask for a quote based on your real footage rather than a generic rate card, and ask when you pay: before the work, or after you approve the final cut.

How reviews and revisions should work

Look for timecoded comments on a review platform such as Frame.io, so you point at the exact frame instead of describing it by email. Agree on the revision policy up front. Collect feedback in one round if you can, so changes don’t contradict each other.
